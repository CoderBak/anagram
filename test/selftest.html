6 · Long paragraph (>1500 chars — the HF-abstract regression)

Modern language model training pipelines increasingly rely on other models at every stage of their construction, using them to generate synthetic data, to filter enormous crawled corpora, to judge candidate outputs during preference tuning, and to guide countless small development decisions that never make it into the final report. These dependencies form a sprawling and largely undocumented graph that connects hundreds of public artifacts across model families, dataset releases, and evaluation suites, and the connections are often visible only through a stray sentence in an appendix, a configuration file in a repository, or an offhand remark in a community discussion thread. Mapping that graph by hand is slow and error-prone, because the evidence is scattered, the naming is inconsistent, and the same artifact frequently appears under several different identities that must be resolved before any structural analysis can begin. An automated approach must therefore read the documentation the way a careful human reviewer would, following references across papers, model cards, and code, while keeping track of exactly which claim came from which source and how confident it is entitled to be about each recovered edge in the graph. The system described here does precisely that, and it matters for this test page for one specific reason: this paragraph is deliberately written to run well past fifteen hundred characters so that any walker which still truncates a unit at a fixed character cap will visibly stop partway through, whereas the correct behaviour is a single badge whose underline runs all the way to the final LONGTAIL sentence at the very end of the paragraph.

WINDOWED-START The keeper's log for that winter runs to nearly four hundred pages, and almost none of it is about the light. It is about weather, mostly, and about the small economies of a household that could be cut off from the mainland for three weeks at a stretch: how much lamp oil was left, how many candles, whether the flour had taken the damp again, which of the hens had stopped laying. He wrote in pencil because ink froze in the well, and he wrote every evening without exception, even on the night his youngest was born in the room below the lantern while a north-easterly took two panes out of the gallery. The entry for that night is eleven words long. It gives the wind, the barometer, the hour the glass went, and then, after a dash, the words "a girl, both well". I have read a great many documents that were meant to move their reader and very few that did it so completely. What strikes me now, going through the pages again, is how little he explained. He assumed a reader who already knew what a gallery was and why losing two panes of it mattered, a reader who could feel the difference between a falling glass at dusk and a falling glass at dawn, and because he assumed that reader he never once stopped to perform his own competence. The modern equivalent would be written very differently. It would open by establishing context, it would define its terms, and it would close each section with a short summary of what had just been said, in case anybody had been skimming. In the middle of the log, however, the handwriting changes, and so does everything else. His eldest son took over the evening entries in February, when the keeper's eyes began to fail, and the son had been to school on the mainland and had opinions about how a record ought to be kept. The sentences get longer and more even. Observations arrive in threes. Each day now begins with a statement of general conditions, proceeds through a list of duties performed, and ends with a remark about the outlook for the following day, and the remark is nearly always that conditions are expected to remain broadly similar, subject to change. It is, in its way, a perfectly good log. It is complete, it is legible, and an inspector from the Board would have preferred it to his father's in every respect. It is also almost impossible to read for more than a page, because nothing in it has been noticed by anybody: the son records that the wind was strong where the father recorded that the hens would not leave the coop, and only one of those tells you how strong the wind was. I do not want to make too much of this. The son was seventeen, he was doing a difficult job in the dark, and he kept the light burning through the worst March on that coast in sixty years, which is the only thing a keeper's log is finally for. But it is hard not to see, in the change of hand, a small rehearsal of an argument we are all now having about what it means for a piece of writing to be good, and whether evenness, completeness and a reliable summary at the end of every section are virtues in themselves or only the things that are easiest to check. The father never summarised anything. He wrote down what he saw, in the order he saw it, and trusted that whoever came after him would be able to tell what mattered from the fact that he had bothered to write it down at all. When the log resumes in his hand, in the last week of April, the entries are shorter than ever, the pencil is blunter, and the first of them says only that the ice had gone out of the harbour overnight and that the supply boat was expected on the morning tide. The boat came, the oil was landed, the girl was christened in May, and the final WINDOWTAIL sentence of the volume records nothing but the wind.
